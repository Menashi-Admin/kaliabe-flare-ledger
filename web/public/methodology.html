Methodology & confidence

The Flare Ledger is built on the principle that honest uncertainty is a feature, not a weakness. Every verdict states what was detected, the floor below which this satellite can no longer resolve a flare, and a confidence score. We never present a derived estimate as a regulatory finding.

Independence note. The Kaliabe Flare Ledger is an independent, open-data project. It is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or reporting for the Nigerian Upstream Petroleum Regulatory Commission (NUPRC), NNPC, or any operator. Penalty figures are derived estimates from the published NUPRC basis and are not official assessments.

1. Detection layer — NOAA VIIRS Nightfire

Flares are detected by NOAA VIIRS Nightfire (VNF), run by the Earth Observation Group at the Colorado School of Mines / Payne Institute. VIIRS sees infrared heat at night and is sensitive only to hot emitters. Gas flares burn hotter than almost any other terrestrial source, so readings with temperatures above ~1,400 K are treated as flare candidates (biomass burning and power plants burn cooler).

Detection floor. VIIRS resolves flare heat, not gas molecules directly. Small or intermittent flares fall below the instrument's resolution or are obscured by cloud — the Niger Delta is cloudy for much of the year. A site with no detection in a given year is therefore labelled unverified / below floor, not "not flaring".

2. Volume estimation — World Bank GFMR calibration

Radiant heat is converted to a gas-flow volume using the calibration published by the World Bank's Global Flaring and Methane Reduction (GFMR) partnership, which anchors satellite radiance to country-level gas-flaring statistics. The volumes on these pages are GFMR's per-flare-site estimates; the underlying method is documented by the World Bank (and builds on the VIIRS Nightfire product and Schuit et al. 2023 for methane hot-spot detection principles).

3. Attribution to operators

Each flare site is assigned to a presumed operating company by geometric / radiative association with field boundaries and operator records in the GFMR metadata. Where no reliable operator is available a site is marked Unattributed. Attribution is indicative, not a legal determination.

4. Verdicts and confidence

VerdictMeaningBase confidence
Verified flareA positive flare volume was detected in the latest VIIRS/GFMR record.0.55–0.98
UnverifiedNo volume in the latest record — below detection floor or cloud-obscured.0.4

Confidence is raised by data coverage — the share of years in the record with a positive detection (zero-volume years count as non-detections, not coverage) — and by a named operator for the site; it is capped at 0.98 because VIIRS volume↔radiance calibration carries intrinsic uncertainty from gas composition and combustion efficiency. Correlation is not perfect; treat every verdict as a direction, not an exact quantity.

Trend

Each site's trend compares the mean flared volume of its most recent three years with the mean of up to five years before them (with fewer than four years of record, the last year is compared with the first). Changes within ±5% are stable; beyond that, rising or falling. A site with no baseline volume but recent detections is new (no percentage is defined); a site with no detections in either window is inactive.

5. Penalty basis — NUPRC 2022 Gazette

The penalty shown is a derived estimate on the basis set by the Flare Gas (Prevention of Waste and Pollution) Regulations (2022): a charge of US$2.00 per 1,000 scf (28.317 m³) of associated gas flared. The ledger converts each site's latest-year volume to US$ and, at an indicative FX snapshot, to ₦. The ₦ figures are display conveniences; actual assessments are made by NUPRC and are not reproduced here.

6. Methane-equivalent loss & CO₂e

Flaring converts gas to CO₂ but also vents some unburnt methane, which is far stronger as a greenhouse gas. We apply the GFMR global calibration ratios (151 bcm flared ≈ 389 Mt CO₂e incl. 46 Mt methane) to each site's volume to estimate CO₂e and methane-equivalent loss. These are order-of-magnitude, perfect-combustion-adjusted estimates — the field's single-blind tests show nobody detects or quantifies every release.

7. Decision-layer derivations method KFL-M 1.1 · 2026-10-16

These are Kaliabe derivations from the GFMR series. They add framing, not data.

  • 3-yr and 5-yr change — the latest-year volume against the volume three (five) years earlier. Undefined (n/a) when the base year had no detection: a percentage on a zero base is meaningless.
  • Peer percentile — the share of the other members of the peer set (all 476 sites, or all 40 operators including “Unattributed”) with a strictly smaller latest-year volume (“larger than N%”). Improving faster than N% ranks the 3-yr change, lower being better, among peers whose change is defined.
  • Cumulative penalty — every year 2012–2025 at the US$2.00 / 1,000 scf rate. This is notional: the 2022 rate did not apply before 2022.
  • Projection — a least-squares line through the last three years (2023–2025), extended three years and clamped at zero. Operator and national projections are fitted to the aggregate series. It is a projection, not a forecast of what any operator will do.
  • Scenario — “reduce flaring by X%” scales the latest-year volume by (1 − X/100). Penalty, CO₂e and methane-equivalent are linear in volume, so they scale identically. A scenario, not a forecast.
  • Watchlist — rising: 3-yr change above the chosen threshold, ranked by absolute volume added; newly detected: first positive volume in the last two years; top decile: 90th percentile or above on latest-year volume.
  • Year player — each year shows that year's GFMR volume; “detected” means a positive volume in that year.

The full method, with every constant and its source, is kept in the repository at docs/METHODOLOGY.md. Every decision brief is stamped with this method version and the data vintage.

8. Limitations

  • Only offshore/onshore sites in GFMR's Nigeria set are shown; the dataset is a subset of total Nigerian flaring.
  • Cloud cover materially under-samples the Niger Delta.
  • Volume↔radiance calibration uncertainty is not fully removable from satellite data alone.
  • Operator composition changes over time; attribution reflects the most recent record.
  • The “Niger Delta” flag is a study rectangle (4.5–8.5° E, 4.5–7.0° N), not the statutory Niger Delta region. Far-offshore fields such as Oso fall outside it.
